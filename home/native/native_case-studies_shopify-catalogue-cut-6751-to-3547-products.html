The problem

The store listed 6,751 products across 58 suppliers, all of them active. Few were the owner's own brand. A visitor could not tell what the store was for, and a catalogue that large makes every other improvement slower: collection pages, search and ad landing pages.

Cutting products on a live store is risky. A deleted product cannot come back with its history, and a product that has been selling should not disappear because it was on a list.

How it unfolded

Export: Every product, as it stood

The whole catalogue was exported with its status, supplier and tags before the first change. This file is the rollback.

Shortlist: 22 suppliers to cut

A shortlist of suppliers was sent to the owner, with product counts per supplier and the tech range kept.

Approve: Written approval

The owner replied in writing, and added that any supplier that had been selling should stay.

Check: The numbers had to match

A script refused to build the cut list unless every supplier name resolved to exactly one supplier and the totals matched what the owner had been told.

Apply: 3,204 products to Draft

In batches, with a verification at the end.

What we did with Claude

Make the promise enforceable

The change only ever wrote one field, the product status, set to Draft. No delete, no archive, no other change. Reversing any one supplier was a filter on the export.

Refuse to run on a mismatch

The 22 names told to the owner were matched against the store's real supplier strings. The script stopped unless the totals reproduced exactly: 22 suppliers, 3,204 products cut, 3,547 kept.

Speed it up without risking it

The first approach changed products one batch at a time, 0.2 to 0.5 products a second. Claude ran the same change in 8 parallel shards, giving each worker its own working files and a plan computed once, so none could touch another's. That reached about 2.6 products a second, five times faster, with no failures.

Handle flaky requests

Shopify sometimes aborts a long multi-change request. Batches of 50 failed almost every time. Batches of 20 worked with the occasional retry, which the script handled itself.

Verify against the live store

At the end, a sample of 250 still-active products showed 0 from the cut suppliers.

What we learned

Make the safe promise the only possible action

When the script can only write Draft, the promise cannot be broken by a bug.

Reconcile with what the client was told

Matching the numbers to the message prevents surprises.

Isolate parallel workers

Speed came from giving every worker its own files, not from pushing harder.
